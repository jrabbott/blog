In a [previous post](/posts/ai-accessibilty-tool/), I wrote about using AI as an accessibility tool. That was about support. About friction. About the practical side of getting words out of my head and into the world.

This one is different. It's about the upside.

Dyslexia makes some things harder. I'm not pretending otherwise. But it's also shaped some of the best parts of how I think. I wouldn't trade those parts away.

## Not a struggle story

I don't romanticise dyslexia. It's real. It's inconvenient. It still frustrates me. I still have moments where I wish reading was effortless.

But it's part of how my brain works. And there's real strength in that. I'm not apologising for it. I'm not selling it as a superpower either.

## I see the big picture first

When I walk into a problem, I rarely start with details. I see the shape of it first. The structure. The flow. The connections between pieces.

Sometimes this means I struggle to explain step one before I've mentally jumped to step ten. That can be awkward in a meeting. But it also means I'm comfortable working with complexity. I can hold a messy system in my head and feel where it's wrong.

In engineering, that matters. I might miss a typo in a document three times. But I'll spot an architectural flaw in minutes. That trade feels fair.

## I think in systems, not sentences

Words can be slow for me. Systems aren't.

I tend to think in patterns. In relationships. In cause and effect. If something breaks, I don't just look at the line of code that failed. I ask what pressure in the system made it fail. What assumption is hiding underneath. What future problem is waiting quietly.

Dyslexia nudged me towards this way of thinking early. When reading is effortful, you look for shortcuts. You learn to infer. You look for structure instead of memorising detail. Over time, that becomes a strength.

## I'm comfortable with uncertainty

Growing up with dyslexia means you get used to not being the fastest reader in the room. You get used to getting things wrong. You get used to finding workarounds.

That builds resilience. It also builds comfort with uncertainty.

I'm not afraid of not knowing. I've lived there. I've had to ask for clarification. I've had to double check. I've had to say, I don't understand this yet.

That mindset is powerful in tech. It makes learning less threatening. It makes feedback less personal. It makes iteration normal. Joining a new codebase used to feel daunting. Now it mostly feels like another puzzle to map.

## I ask better questions

Because I can't always rely on quickly absorbing written information, I rely on understanding.

If something is unclear, I ask. If a requirement feels vague, I push for clarity. If a design feels overly complicated, I ask why. This isn't because I want to be difficult. It's because ambiguity costs me more. So I naturally try to remove it.

The side effect is better conversations. Clearer decisions. Fewer assumptions left unchallenged. A vague ticket that looked fine on a quick read often falls apart once you ask what done actually means.

## I care deeply about communication

When writing is hard, you don't take it for granted.

I care about clarity. I care about structure. I care about whether something makes sense when read by someone tired at the end of the day. I might need tools to help me polish, as I wrote about in my last post. I might reread things more times than most. But the outcome matters to me.

Dyslexia has made me more intentional. And intention shows.

## I notice what others miss

Struggling with text sharpens other senses.

I pick up on tone quickly. On mood. On when someone in a meeting has disengaged. On when a solution technically works but feels wrong. I listen carefully. I look for signals beyond the obvious.

That awareness helps in teams. It helps in leadership. It helps in knowing when to slow down or change direction. I've sat in reviews where the document was solid and the room wasn't. The writing wasn't the problem. Something else was off, and it was worth paying attention to.

## I'm creative because I have to be

Workarounds are creativity in disguise.

If you can't rely on doing things the standard way, you invent your own. You build systems that support you. You automate. You simplify. You rethink. That habit spills into everything.

It makes me more experimental. More open to new tools. More willing to question tradition. Not for the sake of being different. But because I've always had to find paths that fit me.

## I genuinely embrace it

There was a time I saw dyslexia only as a deficit. That framing is incomplete.

It's shaped how I think. How I solve problems. How I work with others. It's made me more empathetic, more adaptable, and more systems focused. That adds up in engineering teams.

I don't apologise for it.

## If you're wired like this too

If you're dyslexic and early in your career, it can feel like you're behind. Often you're just developing a different toolkit.

Notice what comes naturally to you in problems, not just what feels hard on the page. Pay attention to how you solve things differently. Those differences aren't flaws. They are signals.

I'm proud of how my brain works. That took time. It didn't require pretending dyslexia is only a gift.
