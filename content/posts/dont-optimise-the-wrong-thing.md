Every week there is a new tool. A new capability. A new promise that AI will make things faster, cheaper, better. In many ways, it does.

I have been pondering a different question: what if we are just getting better at doing the wrong things?

## Conveyancing

Conveyancing is the legal transfer of property when you buy a home. If you have been through it, you will know how it feels. Slow. Opaque. Frustrating. You feel stuck. You do not understand what is happening. You do not feel in control.

So people turn to AI tools like ChatGPT. They upload documents. They ask questions. They try to understand the process.

It works, at the individual level. People understand more. They feel more confident. More informed. More in control.

Zoom out to the system level and something else happens.

Informed buyers ask more questions. More detailed questions. More frequent questions. They challenge conveyancers more. They request more clarification. Conveyancers, already overloaded, now have more work. More emails. More back-and-forth. More cognitive load.

The system slows down. Even further.

Individuals have more power, and the system gets worse.

## A local optimiser

AI is good at optimising parts of a system. It has no understanding of the system as a whole.

Systems thinker Donella Meadows put it simply: a system is not just a collection of parts. It is the relationships between them. Change one part and you change those relationships, often in ways you did not expect.

## The same pattern elsewhere

The conveyancing example is not unique.

**Healthcare.** In systems like the NHS, getting a GP appointment can be difficult. People use AI to understand their symptoms before they go. They show up informed and prepared. Sometimes also convinced: "I think I have this" or "I read that it might be that." The doctor has to diagnose the patient and undo the diagnosis the patient already believes. Appointments take longer. Fewer patients get seen. The system slows down.

**Software development.** Tools like GitHub Copilot generate code in seconds. Developers write more code. Anyone can write code. Much more code. But that code still needs to be reviewed, maintained, and understood. Senior engineers, the people who understand the whole system, become the bottleneck. Development did not speed up. The constraint moved.

**Customer service.** Companies like Amazon use AI to handle support at scale. Simple questions get answered instantly. When something goes wrong, customers arrive at the human agent already frustrated. They have been through the bot. They have tried to solve it themselves. The issue is more complex. More emotional. Harder to resolve. Human work was not removed. It was concentrated into the hardest interactions.

## The pattern

Across these examples, the same shape appears:

1. Find a point of friction.
2. Apply AI.
3. Optimise it.
4. Create a new problem somewhere else.

AI is a powerful local optimiser. It makes individual steps faster, smarter, more efficient.

But systems do not fail at the level of individual steps. They fail at the level of interactions.

In conveyancing, the bottleneck is not just information. It is coordination, communication, and workload distribution.

In healthcare, it is not just knowledge. It is time, capacity, and trust.

In software, it is not just code creation. It is architecture, maintainability, and shared understanding.

Apply AI without stepping back and you do not fix the system. You amplify it. Sometimes that means amplifying the good. Often it means amplifying the flaws.

## Yesterday's solutions

Peter Senge wrote that today's problems come from yesterday's solutions.

AI is rapidly becoming today's solution to everything. That makes it a likely source of tomorrow's problems too.

## Questions worth asking first

Before applying AI to a friction point, a few reframes can help:

- Not "How do we make this faster?" but "Why does this work this way in the first place?"
- Not "How do we help people do more?" but "What should they be doing less of?"
- Not "How do we scale this?" but "What happens if we do?"

Sometimes the most useful move is not to optimise the system. It is to redesign it.

## Redesign, not just optimise

Imagine conveyancing where:

- Information is shared clearly from the start.
- Everyone sees the same status in real time.
- Questions are reduced, not increased.

That is not an AI problem. That is a system design problem. AI can help, but only if it is applied at the right level: not just making parts better, but making the whole system work better.

AI gives leverage. Systems thinking gives direction. Leverage without direction does not just make you go faster. It makes you get lost, faster.
